# Detailed Methodology

## Study Design

### Research Questions

1. To what extent do access to climate forecasts, trust in climate information, and climate risk perception predict climate adaptation behavior?
2. How do these relationships vary across different national and demographic contexts?
3. Is the perception-action gap in climate adaptation universal or context-dependent?

### Theoretical Framework

This study is grounded in **Protection Motivation Theory (PMT)** (Rogers, 1975), which posits that motivation to act is a joint function of:

- **Threat Appraisal:** Perceived severity and personal vulnerability
- **Coping Appraisal:** Self-efficacy and response efficacy

PMT explains why individuals perceiving high climate risk may fail to adapt if they lack perceived coping capacity.

## Measurement Constructs

### 1. Climate Adaptation Behaviour (CAB)
**Definition:** Actions taken to reduce vulnerability to current or expected impacts of climate change

**Sample Items:**
- "I have modified my daily routines in response to climate change"
- "I have invested in measures to adapt to climate variability"
- "I actively engage in community-based climate adaptation initiatives"

**Reliability:** Cronbach's α = 0.75
**Mean Score:** M = 3.53 (SD = 0.76)

**Interpretation:** 
- Lower scores indicate minimal adaptive behavior
- Higher scores indicate greater engagement in climate adaptation actions

### 2. Access to Climate Forecast Information (ACF)
**Definition:** Individual's ability to obtain and receive climate forecast information from various sources

**Sample Items:**
- "I have access to reliable climate forecast information"
- "I receive climate forecasts through multiple channels"
- "Climate forecast information is easily available to me"

**Reliability:** Cronbach's α = 0.81
**Mean Score:** M = 3.56 (SD = 0.85)

**Interpretation:**
- Reflects digital infrastructure, communication systems, and information dissemination networks
- Higher scores indicate better access to climate services

### 3. Trust in Climate Information (TCI)
**Definition:** Individual's confidence in the credibility, accuracy, and reliability of climate forecasts and the institutions providing them

**Sample Items:**
- "I trust the accuracy of climate forecasts"
- "Climate service providers are reliable sources of information"
- "I have confidence in climate-related recommendations from authorities"

**Reliability:** Cronbach's α = 0.79
**Mean Score:** M = 3.53 (SD = 0.79)

**Interpretation:**
- Reflects institutional credibility and public trust in governance
- Mediated by: past forecast accuracy, transparency, political factors

### 4. Climate Risk Perception (CRP)
**Definition:** Subjective evaluation of probability and damage potential of climate-related harm

**Sample Items:**
- "Climate change poses a serious threat to my livelihood"
- "I am vulnerable to climate-related hazards"
- "Extreme weather events are increasingly affecting my region"

**Reliability:** Cronbach's α = 0.71
**Mean Score:** M = 4.09 (SD = 0.73)

**Interpretation:**
- Highest mean score indicates strong risk awareness
- Perception-action gap: High perception but moderate behavior

## Statistical Analysis

### Analytical Strategy

#### Step 1: Descriptive Analysis
- **Purpose:** Summarize key variables and sample characteristics
- **Methods:** 
  - Central tendency (mean, median)
  - Dispersion (SD, range, IQR)
  - Distribution shape (skewness, kurtosis)
  - Frequency distributions by country and demographics

#### Step 2: Bivariate Correlation
- **Purpose:** Assess pairwise relationships between variables
- **Method:** Spearman's rank-order correlation
- **Rationale:** Non-parametric; appropriate for ordinal Likert data
- **Interpretation:** r values from -1 (perfect negative) to +1 (perfect positive)

**Correlation Strength Guidelines:**
| r value | Strength |
|---------|----------|
| 0.0 - 0.3 | Weak |
| 0.3 - 0.5 | Moderate |
| 0.5 - 0.7 | Strong |
| 0.7+ | Very strong |

#### Step 3: Multiple Linear Regression
- **Purpose:** Assess predictive effects of independent variables on adaptation behavior
- **Dependent Variable (DV):** CAB (Climate Adaptation Behaviour)
- **Independent Variables (IVs):** ACF, TCI, CRP
- **Model Comparison:** Two models estimated

**Model 1: Construct Predictors Only**
```
CAB = β₀ + β₁(ACF) + β₂(TCI) + β₃(CRP) + ε
```

**Model 2: Constructs + Demographic Controls**
```
CAB = β₀ + β₁(ACF) + β₂(TCI) + β₃(CRP) + 
      β₄(Age) + β₅(Gender) + β₆(Education) + 
      β₇(Occupation) + β₈(Country) + β₉(Residence) + ε
```

### Regression Diagnostics

**Assumptions Tested:**

1. **Linearity:** Residuals vs. Fitted values plot
   - Visual inspection for non-random patterns
   - Expected: Random scatter around zero line

2. **Normality:** Q-Q plots and Shapiro-Wilk test
   - Expected: Points on diagonal line
   - Alternative: Histogram of residuals approximately normal

3. **Homoscedasticity:** Scale-Location plot
   - Expected: Random scatter with consistent variance
   - Test: Breusch-Pagan test

4. **Independence:** Durbin-Watson test
   - Expected: Residuals are uncorrelated
   - DW statistic near 2 indicates no autocorrelation

5. **Influential Outliers:** Cook's Distance threshold
   - Expected: No points beyond threshold (typically 1.0)
   - Examined in Residuals vs. Leverage plot

**Results:** All assumptions met. See Figure 1 for diagnostic plots.

### Interpretation of Regression Coefficients

**Beta Coefficient (β):**
- Standardized coefficient indicating effect size
- Interpretation: One unit increase in IV → β-unit change in DV (holding others constant)
- Allows comparison across variables with different scales

**Standard Error (SE):**
- Measures precision of estimate
- Smaller SE = more precise estimate

**t-value:**
- t = β / SE
- Measures how many standard errors away from zero
- Larger |t| = stronger evidence against null hypothesis

**p-value:**
- Probability of observing this result if null hypothesis is true
- p < 0.05: Statistically significant at 5% level
- Significance codes: *** p<0.001, ** p<0.01, * p<0.05, . p<0.10

**R² (Coefficient of Determination):**
- Proportion of variance in DV explained by IVs
- Range: 0 to 1
- Interpretation: Model 1 explains 23.7% of variance in CAB

**Adjusted R²:**
- Penalizes addition of irrelevant variables
- More reliable for comparing models

**F-statistic:**
- Tests overall model significance
- F(p, n-p-1) where p = number of predictors, n = sample size
- p < 0.05 indicates model is statistically significant

## Cross-National Variation Analysis

### Country Selection Rationale

Countries selected to represent:
- **Varying Climate Vulnerability:** From least (Japan) to most (Nigeria, Kenya)
- **Different Institutional Frameworks:** Developed (Japan) to developing (Nigeria, Egypt)
- **Geographic Diversity:** Africa, Asia, multiple climate zones
- **Adaptation Capacity:** Range from high (Japan) to low (Kenya, Nigeria)

### Testing Country Effects

**Method:** Include country dummy variables in Model 2

**Results Show:**
- India (β = 0.577, p < 0.001): Significantly higher adaptation than Egypt
- Kenya (β = 0.556, p = 0.001): Significantly higher adaptation than Egypt
- Nigeria (β = 0.594, p < 0.001): Significantly higher adaptation than Egypt
- Indonesia, Japan: Not significantly different from Egypt (reference)

**Interpretation:** Country-specific factors (governance, institutions, social networks) matter more than individual demographics

## Limitations

### Methodological Limitations

1. **Sampling Design**
   - Convenience sample limits generalizability
   - Cannot draw population inferences
   - Potential selection bias toward educated, digitally-connected respondents

2. **Sample Composition**
   - Over-representation of young adults (72.2% aged 18-24)
   - Urban bias (65% urban vs. 35% rural)
   - May not reflect broader population perspectives

3. **Measurement Issues**
   - **Self-report bias:** Respondents may over/underestimate behaviors
   - **Social desirability:** Tendency to provide socially acceptable responses
   - **Common method variance:** Single questionnaire for all variables
   - **Likert scale limitations:** Ordinal data treated as interval in regression

4. **Cross-Sectional Design**
   - Cannot establish causality (only association)
   - Temporal ordering unclear
   - No control for unmeasured confounders

5. **Geographic Limitations**
   - Six countries studied; results may not generalize to others
   - Climate contexts vary within countries (regional variation not captured)
   - Urban/rural comparison limited by unequal sample sizes

### Specific Limitations by Variable

**Trust Measurement:**
- Does not distinguish trust in different institutions (government, NGOs, private sector)
- Cannot assess trust formation processes

**Access Measurement:**
- Does not measure quality or comprehensibility of information
- May reflect availability but not actual utilization

**Adaptation Behavior:**
- Self-reported measures may not reflect actual behavior
- No objective measures of adaptive actions taken
- May include intentions rather than actual behavior

## Future Research Directions

### Recommended Extensions

1. **Longitudinal Design**
   - Track same respondents over time
   - Observe how behaviors evolve as climate conditions change
   - Establish causal relationships

2. **Mixed Methods Approach**
   - Qualitative interviews to understand trust formation
   - Focus groups to explore perceived barriers to adaptation
   - Case studies of successful/unsuccessful adaptation initiatives

3. **Expanded Measurement**
   - Multi-item measures of trust by institution type
   - Behavioral observations (not just self-report)
   - Psychometric scale development and validation

4. **Theoretical Extensions**
   - Test mediating mechanisms (e.g., perceived efficacy)
   - Examine moderating factors (e.g., income, experience with climate events)
   - Integration with other theories (Social Practice Theory, Diffusion of Innovation)

5. **Geographic Expansion**
   - Include countries with different development levels
   - Focus on specific climate-vulnerable regions (Small Island States, Sahel)
   - Within-country regional comparisons

6. **Policy-Relevant Research**
   - Intervention testing (e.g., trust-building initiatives)
   - Cost-effectiveness analysis of climate communication strategies
   - Evaluation of existing climate service programs
